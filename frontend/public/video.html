Transcript

Cold open

Last summer, at Abu Dhabi airport, about 250 working hours were in the High-risk zone. A midday ban does not cover them.

Small print on screen: Hours that station observations (a derived heat-stress truth) put in the ADOSH High zone, summer 2026, working daytime; 95% interval 192 to 316; airport station, not a worksite; zone margin 7 W/m2 assumed.

The product

This is Stellar Blaze. Every evening it turns tomorrow's forecast into a work-and-rest plan for each crew. The crew lead sees which crew heats up first, and can stop work with one tap, and undo it with another. Each worker gets one sign in their own language. Here it is in Arabic, right to left. And "I feel unwell" takes three taps and sends nothing to anyone.

Small print on screen: Demo site, made-up crews and people; workers never need an account; machine translation, not yet reviewed by a native speaker.

The evidence

We replayed summer 2026 against the airport's own readings, using only forecasts made a day ahead, after writing down how we would be judged. The midday ban alone warns on none of those hours. A fixed threshold catches 92% of them, but raises about 280 hours of false alarms. The regulator's own zones raise about 75, and miss 81 danger hours. Our strict setting misses 46 and raises 180. Every policy trades one for the other, and ours sits on the same line as the zones, not above it. What we add is the dial: the employer chooses where on that line to stand.

Small print on screen: Hour-weighted, REAL weather, assumed crew of 20; the fixed rule is WBGT at or above 32 C on the raw forecast; the regulator's zones are the ADOSH zones on the raw forecast; ours is shown at the protective setting, theta 0.3; at the balanced setting, theta 0.45, ours misses 96.5 hours and raises 73.5 against the regulator's zones' 80.5 and 74.5, with overlapping likely ranges; the counters show exact hours and the receipt shows their 95% intervals.

The price of protection

Protection has a price, and the supervisor sees it. Plan on the raw forecast and a crew still breaks the rules for about 15,000 minutes, with about 20% rest. Plan on the corrected forecast and that falls to about 2,100 minutes, at about 48% rest. Simulated crew, real weather.

Small print on screen: REAL weather + assumed crew: SIMULATED crew: 20 experienced adults, heavy work, 480-minute shifts, 138 scored days; rule-violation minutes 15,225 against 2,140; rest as a share of scheduled minutes 20.5% against 47.8%; the zone engine and the validator do the protecting, the forecast sets how much rest is scheduled.

The day

Fourteenth of June, picked in advance by one rule: the forecast, our range, and what the station recorded. Then a supervisor tries a run that is far too long, and the checker names the article it breaks.

Small print on screen: One day is an illustration, not evidence of skill; the crew is simulated (SIMULATED crew: 20 experienced adults, heavy work).

The boundary

An employer asks for one worker's health record. The answer is no. The attempt is logged, and every worker on the site can see that it was asked.

The promise

The measured next step is a same-day refresh at about 10:00 local, using the morning's readings. In development it cuts false-alarm hours by about 10% to 11% at seven of eight airports. That is development evidence, and it is not in the product yet. A pilot summer, with sensors on site, is how this becomes a health claim.

Small print on screen: Development evidence, leave-one-summer-out, not pre-registered, 23 candidates tried; the seven-airport figure was requested after the verdict (post hoc); a same-day refresh, not day-ahead; not tested at a new airport; not in the product; not yet clinically reviewed; one test summer; derived heat-stress truth; no site sensors and no heat-illness outcomes; the demo is hosted in France Central and designed for UAE North.
